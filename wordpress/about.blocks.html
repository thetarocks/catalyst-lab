ABOUT ME / THE PERSON BEHIND THE NOTEBOOK

Curious about the rules.

I’m a high school student interested in the intersection of computer science, quantitative economics and algorithmic mechanism design.

I want to understand how matching algorithms, incentives and payoff structures shape decisions inside social and economic institutions. Your Catalyst is where I document those questions through computational experiments, simulations and working notes.

WHY CODE?

A rule becomes easier to question when you can run it.

A mechanism can look fair or stable on paper and behave differently once its assumptions change. I’m interested in the gap between a theoretical guarantee and the conditions that make it hold.

The work here includes Python implementations of matching and allocation mechanisms, and browser-based models of market execution, hedging, portfolio risk, financial networks and auctions.

THE WORKING METHOD

  1. State the assumptions.
    What does the mechanism assume about people, information or markets?
  2. Make the rule executable.
    Turn the mechanism into inspectable code and account for its intermediate steps.
  3. Test the boundary.
    Change the inputs, check invariants and look for the point where a claim stops holding.
  4. Leave a trail.
    Publish the source, methods and limitations so someone else can follow the reasoning.

01 / Market design

How should a clearinghouse allocate scarce places when participants have different preferences and eligibility rules? My interests include two-sided matching, Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance, quota interleaving and dynamic vacancy chains.

02 / Computational social choice

How does a voting rule distribute influence, and what happens if a participant can split resources across identities? I’m interested in quadratic voting, Sybil resistance, capital concentration and governance mechanisms in decentralized organizations.

03 / Information economics

How do you fund something that people can benefit from without paying for it? I’m interested in public goods, the free-rider problem, non-excludable information goods and quadratic matching-grant mechanisms.
